Blog Essay June 2025

When the check is cheap

Models will change how people build and execute. Not because they sound smarter. Because some fields can tell, instantly, whether the work was right.

Shryas Bhurat June 2025 · San Francisco

Last year the story was that anyone could generate. This year the story is that some people can execute. The difference is not a bigger vocabulary. It is a loop that can tell a model it was wrong, then let it try again until the work holds.

That loop is why software, math, chips, and games feel like they are moving at a different speed than strategy memos and brand copy. The model is not more magical in those fields. The check is cheaper.

If you want to see how building changes, stop staring at the chatbot. Watch the training recipe. Pre-training, mid-training, and post-training are not lab jargon. They are the new production line for capability. Reinforcement learning is how that line starts to resemble practice instead of recitation.

Three stages, not one model

People still talk about "the model" as if it were a single event. In practice, a frontier system is a sequence. Each stage buys a different kind of competence. Confuse the stages and you will spend money in the wrong place, then wonder why the demo does not survive contact with work.

Pre-training Learn the world as text Internet-scale next-token prediction. This is where language, code patterns, and broad knowledge get compressed into weights. It is expensive, slow to repeat, and surprisingly bad at being carefully right.
Mid-training Patch what the web missed A shorter, higher-quality pass. Labs now anneal on textbooks, math, code, and synthetic drills. Allen AI's OLMo 2 put a name on it: a few percent of the FLOPs, a large share of the capability jump.
Post-training Turn knowledge into behavior Supervised fine-tuning, preference tuning, and reinforcement learning. This is where a base model becomes something you can ship: it follows instructions, uses tools, and, if you have a verifier, gets the answer right.

Pre-training is still the factory. It teaches a model to continue. That is not the same as teaching it to finish a job. A pre-trained model can write a plausible proof, a plausible function, a plausible legal memo. Plausible is a dangerous word. It is the sound of work that has not been checked.

Mid-training is the newer middle. After the long crawl over the public web, labs spend a smaller slice of compute on denser data: proofs, textbooks, high-quality code, synthetic problems designed to close a gap. OLMo 2 is useful because it is honest about the split. Most of the FLOPs still go to pre-training. A short second stage, with better data, can move math and reasoning far more than another pass over noisier internet text. Microsoft's Phi work and OpenAI's public comments pointed at the same idea in 2024. By early 2025 it had a name people could use in a meeting.

Post-training is where products actually appear. Instruction data teaches format. Preference data teaches taste. Reinforcement learning teaches search. If you only do the first two, you get a helpful intern. If you can run the third against a real check, you start to get a junior who can close a ticket.

From agreeable to correct

Reinforcement learning is old. What changed is the reward. For a few years the default reward was a person, or a model trained to imitate a person. That is RLHF: reinforcement learning from human feedback. It is how models learned not to be rude, not to ramble, not to refuse the wrong things. It is also how they learned to sound sure.

RLHF is alignment with a taste. That is valuable. It is a poor substitute for truth. Humans get tired. They prefer fluency. They cannot score a thousand math completions an hour without drifting. A reward model trained on those preferences will optimize for the vibe of a good answer. Vibe does not compile.

RLHF Humans rank answers. A reward model learns those ranks. The policy is pushed toward what people like. Best for tone, safety refusals, and "would I send this email." Weak wherever liking and correctness come apart.
RLVR A program checks the work. Unit tests. Exact numeric answers. Proof checkers. Compilers. The policy is pushed toward what passes. Best where a result can be graded without a debate. Weak where the world has no oracle.
Why both still matter DeepSeek-R1, released in January, showed how far verifier-driven RL can take math and code. OpenAI's o-series, including o3 this spring, made the same bet at a larger scale: more RL, more test-time thinking, more tools in the loop. Labs still finish with preference tuning so the model is usable, not only correct on a benchmark.

The Allen Institute's Tülu 3 paper, in late 2024, gave the method a clean name: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. The idea is older than the acronym. If a task has a success signal you can compute, you do not need a human to grade every try. You sample. You check. You reinforce the traces that passed. The reasoning you see in the output is not always taught directly. It often appears because step-by-step work is what got the reward.

On the record as of June 2025: Tülu 3 and RLVR; DeepSeek-R1, January 2025; OpenAI o3 and o4-mini, April 2025; OLMo 2 mid-training.

That is the shift in how people will build. Prompting a chat model is drafting. Training, or selecting, a model against a verifier is production. The scarce skill is no longer "write a clever prompt." It is "define the check so tightly that practice can happen without you in the room."

A model without a verifier can only rehearse. A model with a verifier can improve.

What a verifier actually is

A verifier is any procedure that can score an output without asking a person to use judgment in the moment. The gold version is automatic, cheap, and hard to game. A compiler is a verifier. A test suite is a verifier. A proof assistant is a verifier. A chess engine's terminal position is a verifier. A bank reconciliation that either balances or does not is a verifier.

Weaker cousins still count, if you are honest about the weakness. A linter is a partial verifier. A type checker is a partial verifier. A schema validator is a partial verifier. A retrieval check that confirms a citation exists is a partial verifier. They do not prove the work was good. They prove a failure mode did not occur. That is still enough to train on, and enough to let a person execute faster.

The industries that will change first are the ones that already live this way. They did not invent verifiers for AI. They invented them because the work was too expensive to trust by eye.

Where the check is strong

  • Software Compilers, types, unit tests, property tests, CI. Code either runs or it does not. This is why coding agents are not a parlor trick. The environment grades them.
  • Mathematics and formal methods Exact answers, Lean and Isabelle checkers, contest problems with known keys. DeepSeek-R1 and the o-series got loud here because the oracle already existed.
  • Semiconductors and EDA Simulation, timing, design-rule checks. A layout that fails DRC is not a matter of taste. Chip teams have been verifier-rich for decades.
  • Games and competitive programming Win, loss, Elo, hidden test cases. The loop is complete. Practice can run overnight.
  • Finance operations Reconciliations, matching, limit checks, backtests against a defined PnL. Not "what should we invest in." The parts of the stack that already close the books.
  • Logistics and operations research Constraint solvers, feasible routes, capacity, SLA clocks. A plan that violates a constraint is rejected before a truck moves.

Where the check is weak

  • Strategy and taste There is no compiler for a brand, a narrative, or a five-year plan. Humans remain the reward model, with all the drift that implies.
  • Most of medicine Lab values and imaging can be checked against a gold standard in narrow slices. Diagnosis, counseling, and care plans usually cannot. A plausible note is not a verified outcome.
  • Law, beyond citation You can verify that a case exists. You cannot cheaply verify that the argument would survive a judge. The missing oracle is the proceeding itself.
  • Management and hiring Delayed, noisy rewards. By the time you know, the policy has already moved on.

How this changes building

The last two years trained a generation of builders to talk to models. The next stretch will train them to put models in environments. That sounds like agents. It is more specific than agents. An agent without a verifier is a tourist. An agent with a verifier is a worker.

In software, this is already visible. You do not need the model to be a senior engineer on the first try. You need tests that fail loudly, a sandbox that can run the patch, and enough samples that one of them passes. The builder's job shifts from writing every line to specifying the contract: types, tests, invariants, evals. The people who can write a tight check will outrun the people who can write a long prompt.

In math and science, the same pattern shows up as tools in the loop. OpenAI's o3 and o4-mini, released in April, do not only "think longer." They call Python, search, and inspect files while they think. Test-time compute is not a vibe. It is extra attempts against a check, including checks the model can run itself. That is execution, not chat.

In chips, logistics, and the mechanical parts of finance, the opportunity is to stop using the model as a narrator and start using it as a proposer inside an existing solver. The verifier is already in production. The model is a new way to search the space those systems already know how to reject.

The inverse is also a warning. If your industry has no cheap check, buying a reasoning model will not give you a reasoning company. You will get faster drafts and more confident errors. Healthcare marketing can adopt gen AI tomorrow. A diagnostic loop cannot, not until the outcome is graded by something other than a plausible paragraph. McKinsey's latest enterprise survey, in March, still found more than 80 percent of organizations seeing no enterprise-level EBIT from gen AI. That is not only a change-management problem. It is a verifier problem wearing a transformation slide.

What extra builders should actually do

The first essay in this series argued that cheaper generation multiplies who is allowed to ship. This is the sequel. Cheaper generation without a check multiplies noise. Cheaper generation with a check multiplies power.

Open models make that concrete. When DeepSeek published R1, the recipe was not a secret handshake. Verifier-driven RL on math and code, then distillation into smaller dense models. Tülu 3 had already shown that RLVR could be an open post-training stage, not only a closed-lab trick. That is how a two-person team inherits a production line that used to belong to a frontier lab: not by pre-training a foundation model, but by wrapping a strong open checkpoint in the right checks.

That is the work we care about at Camou Labs. Privacy-first software still needs models that do something. Compass Local AI is only useful if the local answer is good enough to trust without sending the question away. Personal Eyes is only useful if a description of the scene can be checked against what the camera actually saw. Those are verifier problems, made small enough to hold. The neglected user does not need a larger pre-training run. They need a model that can be run, graded, and kept on the device.

The test

Pre-training will keep getting more expensive at the top, and more available in the open middle. Mid-training will keep turning generic text models into models that can do homework. Post-training, especially RLVR, will keep turning homework into work. RLHF will still be there to make the result fit for people.

The map of who builds, and who merely demos, will follow the map of who owns a check. Software, math, silicon, games, closed-loop operations: those fields can practice at machine speed. Fields that live on judgment will still need humans in the reward. That is not a moral ranking. It is an industrial fact.

The test is simple. If you cannot say what would prove the model wrong, you are not ready to let it execute. If you can, you should stop chatting with it and start putting it to work.
